# BIOGLOW — Innovation Project: local topic options

For the three teams (75969, 72516, 61435), based in **La Cañada Flintridge, CA** (San Gabriel
foothills, next to Angeles National Forest, the Arroyo Seco, and Descanso Gardens).

See [competition-plan.md](competition-plan.md) §4 for the full IP process and rubric notes.

## Rules of thumb

- **Each team picks a different problem** (or a clearly different angle). Identical projects
  across sibling teams read as adult-driven and compete against each other in judging.
- **The prototype can be a model *or* a drawing**, and it does **not** have to function, and it
  does **not** have to be LEGO. Cardboard, foam board, 3D print, annotated poster, or a digital
  mockup all count. Pick the medium that makes a stranger understand the idea in ten seconds.
- The rubric grades a *journey*: Identify → Design (research + a real expert) → Create
  (prototype) → Iterate (share with ≥2 outside people, document what changed) → Communicate
  (5-min live, every member speaks).
- A local, specific problem the team personally connects to scores better than a broad global one.

## Option 1 — Post-fire native habitat recovery

**Problem.** The **Eaton Fire** ignited in Eaton Canyon near Altadena on the evening of
**Jan 7, 2025** and burned **14,021 acres** of foothill chaparral right next to La Cañada
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eaton_Fire),
[NASA Earth Observatory](https://earthobservatory.nasa.gov/images/153821/eaton-fire-leaves-california-landscape-charred)).
When chaparral burns too often, non-native annual grasses (mustard, brome) replace the native
shrubs — many chaparral shrubs take **15–20 years** to mature enough to reproduce, and the
grasses dry out and ignite more easily, shortening the interval between fires. This "type
conversion" is a feedback loop that degrades the ecosystem
([LAist, Jan 2026](https://laist.com/news/climate-environment/la-fires-nature-recovery-year-anniversary),
[Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Los-Angeles-wildfires-of-2025)). A year on, burn
scars show native seedlings and invasive weeds coming back together — the outcome isn't decided.

**Solution directions.** Targeted native seed dispersal on burn scars (seed balls, a
drone-drop hopper); an invasive-weed early-detection map so volunteers pull mustard before it
sets seed; erosion-control wattles pre-seeded with native species.

**Local experts to interview.** Eaton Canyon Nature Center (LA County Parks); Arroyo Seco
Foundation; [Theodore Payne Foundation](https://theodorepayne.org/) (native plant nursery,
Sun Valley); California Native Plant Society, San Gabriel Mountains Chapter; a US Forest
Service Angeles NF botanist.

**Season-theme bridge.** Seed dispersal (missions M02, M14), land restoration (Project Spark).

**Caution.** Some team members may have been evacuated or know families who lost homes. For
some kids this is motivating; for others it's too raw — ask before assigning it.

## Option 2 — Native bees in suburban yards

**Problem.** About **1,600 native bee species** have been recorded in California
([UC / *California Agriculture*, Frankie et al.](https://californiaagriculture.org/article/109170.pdf)).
More than a quarter of North America's 47 bumble bee species are at risk of extinction, and
Xerces has petitioned California to list four — including **Crotch's bumble bee**, a Southern
California species — as endangered
([Xerces Society](https://www.xerces.org/endangered-species/wild-bees)). The biggest driver is
habitat loss. Suburban yards planted with non-native ornamentals give native bees little to
forage on and nowhere to nest; studies find bees are more attracted to California native plants
than to exotics, and diversity is highest where many bee plants bloom at once
([Journal of Pollination Ecology](https://pollinationecology.org/index.php/jpe/article/view/505)).

**Solution directions.** A "pollinator patch" starter kit of native plants chosen for
year-round bloom; ground-nesting bee habitat (most native bees nest in bare soil, not hives);
a neighborhood map of forage gaps to connect isolated wild patches.

**Local experts to interview.** [Descanso Gardens](https://www.descansogardens.org/)
horticulturist (in La Cañada Flintridge — does education outreach); Theodore Payne Foundation;
CNPS San Gabriel Mountains Chapter; a biology instructor at PCC or Cal Poly Pomona.

**Season-theme bridge.** Understory pollination; keystone species.

**Why it's the safe pick.** Easiest local expert to reach, cheap prototype, every kid can
plant something and observe results.

## Option 3 — Coast live oak decline

**Problem.** The **goldspotted oak borer (GSOB)**, an invasive beetle first found in California
around 2004 and attacking **coast live oak** (*Quercus agrifolia*) since 2008, has killed tens
of thousands of oaks across Southern California and is established in Los Angeles County
([Western IPM Center](https://westernipm.org/stories/natural-areas/gold-spotted-oak-borer-threatens-oak-woodlands-and-ecosystems-across-southern-california/),
[NRPA](https://www.nrpa.org/blog/protecting-oak-trees-battling-the-goldspotted-oak-borer/)).
It spreads mainly when people **move infested firewood**
([Don't Move Firewood](https://www.dontmovefirewood.org/pest_pathogen/goldspotted-oak-borer-html/)).
Built-up Los Angeles holds an estimated **250,000–300,000 coast live oaks**, with ~92,000 acres
of single-family homes shaded by them — La Cañada's heritage oaks among them.
[UCLA's Institute of the Environment](https://www.ioes.ucla.edu/project/monitoring-goldspotted-oak-borer-gsob-spread-in-southern-california-with-imaging-spectroscopy)
is tracking the spread from the air.

**Solution directions.** A homeowner early-detection checklist / photo guide (D-shaped exit
holes, crown dieback, woodpecker activity); a local "buy it where you burn it" firewood
campaign; a simple sticky-trap monitoring design for a neighborhood.

**Local experts to interview.** LA County Agricultural Commissioner / Weights & Measures; UC
Agriculture & Natural Resources (UC ANR) oak specialist; the City of La Cañada Flintridge urban
forester (the city protects native oaks); a UCLA IoES researcher on the monitoring project.

**Season-theme bridge.** **Keystone Species (mission M13)** — oaks are the classic keystone
species; hundreds of species depend on a single mature oak.

## Option 4 — Foothill wildlife connectivity

**Problem.** In **La Cañada Flintridge**, a black bear was struck and killed on the **210
freeway** ([CBS Los Angeles](https://www.cbsnews.com/losangeles/news/car-hits-kills-bear-on-210-freeway-in-la-canada-flintridge/)).
Freeways and busy foothill roads act as near-impenetrable barriers: GPS and camera-trap data
show cougars, bobcats, gray foxes, coyotes, and mule deer walk up to highways but rarely cross
([NRDC](https://www.nrdc.org/bio/damon-nagami/wildlife-corridors-crucial-californias-biodiversity)).
The **Wallis Annenberg Wildlife Crossing** over US-101 at Liberty Canyon (≈210 ft long, 165 ft
wide) shows the fix works — mule deer used it before it was even finished — but crossings only
help when fencing funnels animals to them and away from traffic
([Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/mule-deer-are-already-using-californias-first-wildlife-crossing-and-its-not-even-finished-yet-180988933/),
[Undark](https://undark.org/2026/06/12/california-wildlife-crossing/)).

**Solution directions.** A camera-trap survey of likely crossing points on local roads (Angeles
Crest Highway, Foothill Blvd, the 210 on-ramps); a driver-alert signage proposal for known
hotspots; a culvert-retrofit pitch to the city or Caltrans.

**Local experts to interview.** Pasadena Audubon; National Wildlife Federation
(#SaveLACougars); Caltrans District 7 biologist; an NPS Santa Monica Mountains researcher (for
camera-trap methodology).

**Season-theme bridge.** Camera traps and animal-monitoring tools (Project Spark; mission M09).

## Option 5 — Light pollution and night wildlife

**Problem.** **Mount Wilson Observatory**, in the Angeles National Forest directly above La
Cañada and Pasadena, is now the **most light-polluted of 28 major astronomical observatories in
the Western world**; the LA area has grown from ~500,000 people when it was built to ~18.6
million today
([UPI](https://www.upi.com/Voices/2025/07/29/astronomy-light-pollution-worsens/5221753795300),
[The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/light-pollution-is-encroaching-on-observatories-around-the-globe-making-it-harder-for-astronomers-to-study-the-cosmos-260387)).
The same skyglow harms wildlife: under street lighting, ground-level **moth abundance is halved,
species richness is >25% lower**, and nocturnal **pollen transport is disrupted** — the first
evidence that light pollution damages the pollination process itself, not just moth numbers
([Science Advances, 2021](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.abi8322);
[Global Change Biology, Macgregor et al. 2017](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/gcb.13371);
[Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/nocturnal-pollinators-go-dark-under-street-lamps/)).
Moths are in long-term decline and are key prey for bats and birds.

**Solution directions.** A shielded, warm-color "wildlife-friendly" outdoor light fixture
(design + model); a cheap sky-brightness sensor to map the darkest remaining pockets; a
dark-sky lighting proposal for an HOA, a school campus, or the city.

**Local experts to interview.** [Mount Wilson Observatory](https://www.mtwilson.edu/) staff;
[DarkSky International](https://darksky.org/) Los Angeles advocates; a Caltech astronomer; the
city's planning department (lighting ordinance).

**Season-theme bridge.** The tightest fit to **BIOGLOW** — bioluminescence, moths, "the forest
that glows"; the story of natural night light versus artificial light.

## How to run the choice (next session)

Give each team the shortlist. 20 minutes: which one do we *care* about, and why us? Then pick.
If two teams want the same topic, the tiebreaker is which team already has a personal
connection. Their answer to "why does this matter to us" is a scored rubric line — it has to be
theirs, not the coach's.

## Running the research phase — adult guidance vs. kid ownership

Adults teach the *how*; kids do the finding, reading, and thinking. Coaching a skill is
expected — handing over a finished product is what judges penalize.

| Adults do | Adults don't |
|---|---|
| Teach how to search, judge a source, take notes | Hand over articles with the key points highlighted |
| Give a **small curated starter set** ("3 local orgs + 1 overview article") | Give the synthesized findings or conclusions |
| Sit beside asking "what makes you trust that page? who wrote it?" | Type the notes / write the summary |
| Draft the expert email *with* them; send from the coach account | Do the interview while kids watch |
| Prep interview questions together | Decide what the problem and solution are |

**Teach it as short mini-lessons across the Design-phase sessions** (9–14-year-olds mostly
can't research well yet):

1. *Searching* (15 min) — keywords not questions; try the org's own site; what `.gov` /
   `.edu` / `.org` vs. a random blog tells you.
2. *Judging a source* (15 min) — who wrote it, are they an expert, when, do others agree.
   Practice on two pages about the same fact, one strong and one weak.
3. *Note-taking* (ongoing) — one fact per row with its source. Becomes the presentation and
   the Q&A answers.
4. *Finding an expert* (15 min) — local org → "staff / about / education" page → contact form.
5. *Outreach email* (20 min) — who we are, what we're doing, 2–3 specific questions, how
   little time we're asking. Kids draft; coach polishes tone.
6. *The interview* (10 min prep) — record only with permission; one asks, one takes notes;
   listen and follow up rather than reading a script.

**Youth Protection:** external contact is adult-facilitated — the email goes from the coach's
account (or coach CC'd) and an adult is present for the interview (in person or on the call).
Judges know this is normal; it doesn't reduce the "students did the research" credit as long
as the kids wrote and asked the questions.

**What judges reward:** they ask the kids directly — "How did you find this? What surprised
you? Who did you talk to?" A team that answers in its own words, including "we thought X, then
the ranger told us Y," scores full marks even with modest sources. A polished bibliography the
kids can't discuss scores poorly.

## Reference presentations to show the team

Community-posted examples — varying quality, not official standards. Several run ~6 min
(older format allowed more); the current limit is a strict **5 minutes**, so watch for
*structure*, not length. Have the kids watch 2–3, then fill in the "what to watch for" grid
below together.

| Video | What it is | Why watch |
|---|---|---|
| [LOAD Robotics — Innovation Project, SUBMERGED](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j9a4GBtQWTM) (~6 min) | Strong competitive team (Core Values Finalist, 2024–25 World Championship) | Clean structure: problem → research → solution → prototype → sharing → impact |
| [Team Turtlebots — Innovation Project, SUBMERGED, 2025 Georgia State Championship](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ydyyjkzJInU) (~6 min) | State-championship-level team presentation | Pacing, visuals, how they name their expert contacts |
| [LEGO Designer 101 — Vermont Champions Innovation Project](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TRnj6t-RGvw) (~3.5 min) | Tighter, shorter presentation | Shows a short version can still cover every rubric line |
| YouTube search: `"[season] innovation project presentation"` | Dozens of team uploads each season | Compare a range — the good ones all hit the same beats |
| [FLLTutorials — Project section](https://flltutorials.com/en-us/Project) | Written guides + "features of a good presentation" PDF | Checklist form; pairs with the rubric |

**What to have the kids watch for** (map each to a rubric line):

- Problem stated clearly in the first 30 seconds, with why *this* team cares
- Evidence of real research and at least one **named** expert
- Solution explained simply, with the prototype shown and pointed at
- A clear "we shared it, got feedback, and changed X" moment (iteration)
- **Every team member speaks** a real segment
- Ends on impact — who this helps and how
- Engaging delivery (story, skit, or demo) that still puts substance first

**Warning:** don't copy a format wholesale. Judges see the same skit structure over and over;
a presentation that's obviously modeled on a YouTube video loses the authenticity points. Use
the examples to understand the *beats*, then let the team tell its own story its own way.
